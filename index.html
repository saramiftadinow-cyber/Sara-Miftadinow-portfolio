Vectra · TV Smart

TV Smart.

TV Smart by Vectra is a comprehensive streaming platform available across four surfaces: TV decoder, Smart TV app, responsive web, and mobile app.

Duration

2023 – 2025

My role

Lead UX/UI Designer & Researcher

Platforms

TV decoder · Smart TV app · Web (desktop + mobile) · Mobile app

Case study

Optimising the login & registration flow

This case study focuses on redesigning the login and registration flow for the web application, covering both desktop and mobile versions.

Duration

Q2 2023

Team

UX/UI Designer (me), with Vectra stakeholders & front-end team

Platform

Web (desktop + mobile)

Tools

Figma · Adobe XD · Photoshop · Jira · Confluence · Miro

01

Business context

The project started with a concrete business problem: a low percentage of users were completing the login process, and many were calling operator helplines because they couldn't navigate onboarding. Product analytics confirmed a low completion rate. Operator helplines simultaneously reported a high volume of calls from users who didn't know how to proceed.

TV Smart serves two distinct user types. Subscription users register and log in directly through the TV Smart platform. Operator users are customers of separate partner companies, Vectra, Multimedia, Jambox, and Elsat, who received access to TV Smart as part of their operator package. These users have their own dedicated login path, using credentials from their operator's system (e.g. customer number, eBOK login), not a TV Smart account. This distinction is fundamental to understanding why the login flow is complex and why a single generic form failed both groups.

For the business this meant: lost user activations, rising support costs, customer frustration, and a negative first product experience. We knew a problem existed, but not why users were dropping off.

The project covered the full cycle: usability tests → diagnosis → redesign → mockup validation → implementation → follow-up usability testing.

02

UX audit

Before user research, I conducted a comprehensive UX audit of the existing login and registration process, identifying points where the architecture didn't support natural user behaviour.

The audit revealed multiple friction points: inconsistencies between system logic and user expectations, blocking validations instead of guiding users, artificial separation of login and registration, error messages that didn't help users decide, and missing contextual cues.

Example: social login edge case

A logged-out user on the registration screen could click social login. The system blocked them with a "no account" error instead of recognising the intent and guiding them forward. Technically correct, experientially, an unnecessary obstacle. The process was designed around system structure, not user mental models.

Before, existing login screen

Dark modal combining login and registration with a tab toggle, high cognitive ambiguity, social login low in the hierarchy, poorly visible operator path.

03

Usability research

I conducted usability tests with 10 participants, 5 operator users and 5 subscription users. During sessions I observed moments of hesitation, login path choices, reactions to errors, and decisions to restart the process.

Operator users (5)

Didn't notice the operator tab. Entered operator credentials into the TV Smart form. Received a generic "invalid credentials" error with no indication of the cause. Made repeated attempts instead of switching path. The error was interpreted as a password problem, not a process selection problem.

Subscription users (5)

Preferred social login and ignored the traditional form when an alternative existed. Registration requiring sign-up then re-login felt long and unintuitive. Extra steps raised doubts about whether the system was working. Onboarding needed to be as frictionless as possible.

Key insight

Users didn't understand the system's structure, and the interface wasn't helping them make the right decision.

04

Flow documentation

Before designing, I mapped every authentication scenario, email login, social login (Google, Facebook, Apple), operator login for all four operators, password recovery, and registration with both email and social paths. No undefined edge cases, no flows invented mid-development.

Complete authentication flow map, every login path, registration variant, and edge case documented before design began.

05

Key design decisions

Based on research insights, the focus wasn't on reducing features, it was on changing the architecture of choice. We applied progressive disclosure: the most common path is visible immediately, other options don't create cognitive overload.

Social login elevated as default, before & after

Research showed subscription users preferred and actively sought social login. We moved it to the primary position, top of the form, to match actual usage patterns, reduce friction, and shorten the onboarding path for the majority of new users.

Before

After

Dedicated operator forms, one per operator

Vectra, Multimedia, Jambox, and Elsat each have different credential systems. A single generic form couldn't serve them all. Each operator now gets a dedicated form with their logo, contextual field labels matching their terminology, and "forgot password" links redirecting to their own portal.

Intent recognition, system adapts to user action

Previously if a user tried to log in without an account, the system blocked them with a generic error. Now the system recognises the intent and guides them forward automatically. Users don't need to understand which screen they're on, the process adapts to their action.

Registration as 2 clear steps

Step 01: account creation. Step 02: legal consents. Separating GDPR from credentials makes the legal step feel like a natural checkpoint rather than friction.

White form, a deliberate trust signal

Data from other services showed white forms generate more user trust in authentication flows, contrary to the dark-form convention common in streaming. We made this deliberate choice to differentiate the onboarding experience at the moment of maximum hesitation.

06

Final designs

07

Follow-up testing & results

After launch, I ran a second round of moderated usability tests on the live product, with the same task scenarios and participant mix as the first round, to directly compare the new flow against the baseline.

9/10

Completed login unassisted, vs 5/10 in round one

0

Process restarts observed, vs 7 across round-one sessions

~2×

Faster task completion in moderated sessions

A note on quantitative validation

We couldn't validate the redesign with an A/B test, product analytics weren't instrumented on the login flow at the time. Follow-up usability testing was the most reliable evidence available. Setting up event-level analytics for the flow became one of my key recommendations after the project.

Key lessons

The project revealed that low login effectiveness was the result of multiple overlapping problems: flow structure, error messages, and rigid separation of registration from login. The biggest improvement came from removing moments where the system blocked users instead of guiding them forward.

Users don't analyse processes, they react to what they see on screen. Error messages directly influence the decision to abandon. The results were very well received by stakeholders, and the project became the starting point for planning the same redesign in the mobile app.

Case study

Optimizing and increasing conversion on mWeb

Despite strong desktop performance, the mobile web experience was generating significant drop-off, and no one had looked at the full journey holistically. This case study is about changing that.

Duration

Q2 2025

Team

Product trio: Product Designer (me), Researcher, Product Owner

Platform

Mobile web (mWeb)

Tools

Figma · Miro · Jira · Confluence · Teams · Chat GPT

01

Problem

Collaboration: product trio (designer, me, researcher, product owner)

We noticed that mWeb has significantly more users than desktop, almost 2x more. At the same time, conversion, meaning clicks on "Apply" for offers, is lower than on desktop, by almost half. We decided this needed a closer look: to check why this was happening and what we could do to change it. We asked ourselves: what can we do better to increase conversion on mWeb?

02

Analysis & planning

We went back to our earlier research, Continuous Discovery on pracuj.pl mWeb, where we had already spotted a few critical moments to improve, as well as things that could better serve users on mWeb. Based on that, we ideated solutions and built a prototype for the follow up study we planned.

The study

Goal: verify our ideas, look for opportunities to improve the user experience and increase conversion on mWeb.

Respondents: 10 people from the White, Pink, Blue and IT segments, aged 25 to 35, proficient mobile phone users.

Methods: in-depth IDI interviews, usability testing on prototypes. On the prototype, we presented several ideas responding to our users' needs.

03

What we tested

Home page, Pracuj Stories

Problem: "I feel like I'm seeing the same things over and over again, just in a different order. I'd like a bit more variety, something new."

Proposal: a Stories-style module on the home page, a format that works well on many websites and apps, and could keep users around longer and engaged with recommendations.

Response: users liked the section a lot and said they could see themselves staying longer.

Login, visible status

Problem: "When I go to Pracuj, I don't know if I'm logged in."

Proposal: a simple solution, on the bottom bar, next to the user icon, we added a "Log in" label visible for users who aren't logged in.

Response: users could immediately tell whether they were logged in or not.

Search, AI query & voice

Problem: "I'm afraid I might miss an interesting offer because of incorrect searching."

Proposal: an AI query mode alongside the classic search.

Response: mixed reception, some users trust the classic search and filters more, others really liked the new solution.

Location, beyond city level

Problem: "I want to be able to specify a district or mark an area where I could search."

Proposal: saved locations, radius selection, search by district.

Response: users enjoyed browsing on the map and saving locations, letting them search faster without typing a full address each time.

List of offers, AI summary

Problem: "I don't want to read long ads. I want to be able to quickly decide whether it's worth reading the offer in detail or not."

Proposal: short offer summaries directly on the tiles in the listing.

Response: found very helpful and useful, but users felt the format could be a short bullet-point list with key facts instead of a text block.

Offer, sliding drawer

Problem: "I always open offers in new tabs and then go back to the list. It's awkward on a small screen. I get lost."

Proposal: offer details open in a sliding drawer instead of loading a whole new page.

Response: much more convenient, the screen doesn't reload and they land back in the exact same spot on the list, staying in context the whole time.

Recommendations, "For You" tab

Problem: "I've looked through all the search results. I feel like there are more offers on the website that could potentially interest me."

Proposal: a "For You" tab with a swipe mechanic for recommended offers, a fresh new way to browse.

Response: a fun, novel idea users hadn't expected, they said they would happily use it, wanting assurance they wouldn't see offers they'd already viewed or applied to.

04

Key findings

The research surfaced a few interesting insights.

Browse on mobile, apply on desktop. For most users in the White and IT segments, and some in Pink and Blue, mWeb is used for casual browsing: scrolling through offers during the commute, saving interesting ones to favorites, then returning to them later on desktop to read the details, edit their CV, and apply.

Users are increasingly aware that their CV goes through an ATS that scans it for keywords matching the offer. If it doesn't hit the right words, the CV can be rejected before a human ever sees it. This awareness pushes people to apply from a setup where they can be careful, rather than from a phone during the commute.

Blue segment candidates behave the opposite way. Pressed for time and worried offers will disappear quickly, they apply or call right away, using a single universal CV they don't edit for each offer.

mWeb isn't a worse version of the application flow, it's a completely different job. For most users it's about discovery and shortlisting, not completion.

05

Next steps

01

Product trio agreed that mWeb should primarily serve users for quick, easy browsing and discovery of offers.

02

Impact/effort workshop, prioritizing what to implement first based on business value and development effort, while flagging cross-team topics with other teams.

03

High-fidelity design of the final solutions.

04

Development.

05

Rollout and A/B testing.

06

Gathering findings, then iteration.

AioCare · Health tech · 2020–2022

A system for monitoring and treating lung diseases.

A professional system for monitoring and treating lung diseases, combining a portable spirometer integrated with mobile apps and an online dashboard for accessing results.

Duration

Aug 2020 – Sep 2022

My role

Lead UI/UX Designer

Team

Product owner · PM · iOS/Android/front-end/back-end devs · UI/UX designers · testers

Target audience

Patients with lung diseases and their treating doctors

01

Business context

Joining the AioCare project, I found two existing applications already in place: one for patients and another for doctors. The business goal was to create a comprehensive web application based on several existing screens, alongside plans to expand the mobile apps' functionality and, at a later stage, carry out a complete redesign to enhance usability.

In the meantime, I focused on developing a cohesive design system and redesigning the entire set of medical reports, as lead UI/UX designer responsible for both the web application and the mobile apps' interfaces.

Two applications, patient and doctor, needed to feel like one coherent system, without forcing every screen into an identical mold.

02

Design system

While developing new, refactored screens, I initiated the creation of a unified design system for both applications, split into Patient and Doctor sections so each could carry its own color identity while sharing the same underlying components.

Foundations

Definitions of global styles: typography, colors, and layout grids.

Components

Patient and Doctor variants, differentiated mainly by color scheme.

iOS UI kit

Platform-specific components aligned with Apple's interface guidelines.

Auxiliary objects

Ready-made objects for labeling and tagging screens during flow construction.

Alongside the design system, I created flowcharts mapping the application processes, documentation that helped identify issues early and kept the team aligned on how each flow was supposed to work.

03

Case study: profile creation

Creating patient and doctor profiles is a crucial step in the system registration process. These profiles include essential information such as personal details, and for patients, additional lifestyle information vital for effective health monitoring. The objective: redesign and optimize the existing pathways for creating both profile types.

I began by gathering requirements and defining the exact content the pathways needed to collect, then analyzed the current profile creation flow and reviewed pathways from other applications for best practices and inspiration.

Problem definition

Empathy-mapping workshops with the project team surfaced the main issues: an unattractive interface, and content that needed rewriting. Both made the pathway feel like a chore rather than a natural first step into the product.

04

Research

Research focused on how other product teams and competing companies approach profile creation, what information they collect, and in what order. It also fed specific features directly, such as the pattern used for drop-down menus.

Key insight

The problem wasn't missing functionality, it was an interface and content layer that made a simple task feel effortful.

05

Key design decisions

Fewer steps, fewer questions

Reduced the number of steps and questions required to create a profile by eliminating unnecessary information and keeping only essential data.

A more logical structure

Redesigned the interface layout to improve navigation and create a clearer, more logical flow of information through both the patient and doctor pathways.

Content revision

Rewrote the copy throughout the pathway to make it clearer and easier to read at the moments where users had previously hesitated.

A more attractive interface

Introduced a modern, aesthetically pleasing design that aligns with the rest of the application, addressing the visual issue raised in empathy mapping directly.

Mockups were prepared for both the patient and doctor applications, then turned into prototypes illustrating individual transitions and animations, a key piece of documentation for developers during implementation.

06

Workshops, testing & results

I ran workshops with the project team to discuss and refine design ideas, gather feedback, and check alignment with user needs and project goals, then performed hallway testing to validate the redesigned profiles and collect data for further improvements. The process included iterations, plus support for developers during implementation.

Faster profile creation

The profile creation process is now significantly faster and more efficient, saving users time.

Improved intuitiveness

A more intuitive, easier-to-navigate interface, simplifying account creation and enhancing the overall experience.

Vectra · Enterprise · 2023

An internal CRM designed end-to-end.

A CRM system for Vectra employees enabling efficient order management and quick access to customer information, with activity tracking, personalized tasks, and recommendations that streamline daily operations.

Duration

Jun – Sep 2023

My role

UX/UI Designer, full ownership of UX & UI

Team

Analyst · Full-Stack Developer

Tools

Figma · Material UI · Jira · Confluence

01

Objectives

Design a new CRM system to support Vectra employees in their daily tasks, functional, intuitive, and accessible across web, mobile, and tablet. The system was designed independently, with no reference to the existing CRM, as per instructions. To meet tight deadlines, the project used the Material UI component library for efficient implementation.

The main challenge: simplifying Vectra's complex processes and product offerings so the system stayed intuitive, functional, and scalable.

02

My role

Full responsibility for the UX and UI of the system, designed from scratch to meet client requirements and the needs of Vectra's employees and business operations.

Requirements analysis

Understanding the needs and goals of users and stakeholders.

Defining features

Identifying key functionalities to streamline daily tasks.

Wireframes & prototypes

Simple, user-friendly solutions for the complexity of Vectra's processes and products.

Iteration & scalability

Improvements based on user and team feedback; designed to allow adding new features over time.

03

Process

01

Requirements gathering

Detailed discussions with the analyst (also acting as PM) to understand business goals, user needs, technical assumptions across web, mobile, and tablet, plus timeline and budget.

02

Documentation analysis

A deep dive into Vectra's package descriptions, their dependencies and pricing, and the functional requirements needed to handle that complexity.

03

Workshops

Sessions with Vectra employees and business representatives, observing how they use the current CRM, gathering feedback, and building relationships with the client.

04

Collaboration

Many hours of discussion with the analyst: business goals, package specifics, platform analysis, and joint problem-solving through brainstorming.

05

Competitor analysis

Research across the CRM market to learn current trends and best practices, and to draw inspiration from other systems' functionalities and interfaces.

06

Wireframes & iteration

Initial wireframes reviewed with the team and refined through multiple iterations, supported by end-user interviews and stakeholder workshops.

07

Final design

Mockups turned into a complete, ready-to-implement system, with final testing and evaluation before release.

04

Workshop insights

Easy for new employees. The system should be intuitive, understandable from day one, and clarify the complexity of the packages.

More than sales. Users needed additional customer service and reporting functionalities beyond the core flows.

Multi-platform. Part of the team works in the field, so the CRM had to work on web, mobile, and tablets.

05

Outcome

After many iterations and continuous refinement, the mockups became a complete, ready-to-implement CRM system.

Tailored to user needs

An intuitive interface, functional tools, and customized business processes.

Built on proven patterns

Material UI and established design patterns for stability, performance, and fast implementation.

Integration-ready

Designed to connect with existing IT systems, streamlining information flow and removing manual data entry.

Meets business goals

Better work organization, higher productivity, and improved customer service.
